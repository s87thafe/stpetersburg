\section{Introduction}

The St. Petersburg paradox was first introduced by Daniel Bernouilli in 1738 \citet{bernoulli1954exposition}.
Its core principle is a simple game of coin tosses. A fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads.
The payoff of the game is determined by the number of tosses it takes to get heads.
If it takes $n$ tosses to get heads, the player receives a payoff of $2^n$ dollars.
As the expected value of the game is indefinite, a rational player who is maximizing expected value should be willing to pay any finite amount to play.
This clearly does not hold in practice.

To motivate the preceding concepts, let us consider the so-called St. Petersburg paradox. A
fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads for the first time. If the game stops after $k$ tosses,
you are paid $2^k$. This results in the following lottery:

\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
\text{Payoff} & \$2 & \$4 & \$8 & \cdots & \$2^k & \cdots \\
\text{Probability} & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{4} & \frac{1}{8} & \cdots & \frac{1}{2^k} & \cdots
\end{array}
\]

The expected payoff of this lottery is
\[
2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 8 \cdot \frac{1}{8} + \cdots
= 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots = \infty.
\]
So, if you are maximizing your expected payoff, you should be willing to pay any amount of
money to participate in the game. Since, empirically, most people would not do this, expected
payoff maximization does not seem to be a common objective in this situation. \citet{bernoulli1954exposition}
suggested that people maximize expected utility rather than payoff. If utility increases
sufficiently slowly in money, then the expected utility of the game is finite.
For example, suppose the utility of $n$ is $\log_2 n$. Then, the game has expected utility
\[
1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 3 \cdot \frac{1}{8} + \cdots = 2.
\]

While this resolves the original paradox, it does not resolve the problem for unbounded utility functions in general. \citet{menger1934unbounded} shows that for any utility function unbounded from above, one can construct a lottery with infinite expected utility. Formally, let $u$ satisfy $\sup_x u(x) = \infty$. Then for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$ there exists an outcome $x_n$ with $u(x_n) \geq 2^n$, and the lottery $L$ paying $x_n$ with probability $2^{-n}$ yields
\begin{align*}
    \mathbb{E}[u(L)] = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{-n} \, u(x_n) \geq \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{-n} \, 2^n = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1 = \infty.
\end{align*}
For Bernoulli's logarithmic utility, $u(x) = \ln x$, choosing $x_n = e^{2^n}$ gives
\begin{align*}
    \mathbb{E}[\ln L] = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{-n} \ln\!\left(e^{2^n}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1 = \infty.
\end{align*}
Finite expected utility for every lottery with countable support therefore requires $u$ to be bounded \citet{arrow1974unbounded}.
